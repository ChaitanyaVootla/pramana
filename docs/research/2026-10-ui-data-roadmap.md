# UI patterns, open data and content roadmap (research, Oct 2026)

Research notes for the atlas-and-chapters reader page, data expansion and the next eras. Written 2026-10-17. Nothing here is decided. Each recommendation names its evidence, and points that rest on vendor docs or secondary summaries are marked as such.

**Short version**

- Keep the atlas **reader-driven**: scrolling emphasises, it never moves the camera or the year. Add one opt-in "follow the text" mode. The scrubber should snap to the years where the borders actually change.
- **Be honest about borders.** Cliopatria encodes no uncertainty, and from about 1800 it **folds the princely states into British territory**. It has **no polities at all between 1700 and 600 BCE** (the Vedic period), and it draws the Indus civilisation as one 755,000 km² "polity". Each of these needs a UI answer, not just a dashed line.
- **Data you can use:** Cliopatria (CC BY 4.0), OpenHistoricalMap (CC0, thin for India), Chronas data (CC BY-SA 4.0), Pleiades (585 places in the subcontinent), Wikidata (4,009 ASI "Monuments of National Importance", 2,963 with coordinates and 1,863 with an image), and the Imperial Gazetteer 1907–09 maps on Commons (48 files, PD). **Do not use:** GeaCron ("All Rights Reserved"), Euratlas (paid, no redistribution), Schwartzberg's atlas on DSAL (© Schwartzberg), Davies' 1949 atlas (in copyright, despite CC0 tags on archive.org uploads), or historical-basemaps unless you accept GPL-3.0 for the data.
- **Next eras, in order:** Indus → Vedic → Mahājanapadas and the Buddha → (Maurya, done) → Gupta (in progress) → early medieval South (Cholas) → Delhi Sultanate → Vijayanagara → Mughal → Marathas → Company rule → (1857–1947, done). The reasons are in §3.
- **Learning-science quick wins:** a pre-question per chapter, refutation-structured misconception blocks, a confidence rating before the answer is revealed, timeline anchors to things the reader already knows, and a two-minute retrieval check at each chapter's end.

**The evidence on scroll-driven vs reader-driven is thin.** Kosara argues that continuous scroll mismatches discrete story steps, hides the story's length and blocks direct access. He prefers a stepper ([The Scrollytelling Scourge](https://eagereyes.org/blog/2016/the-scrollytelling-scourge)). A 240-person study found that **transition and feedback design affected engagement, while the level of control (discrete vs continuous) "may not"** ([Visual Narrative Flow, MSR](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/visual-narrative-flow-exploring-factors-shaping-data-visualization-story-reading-experiences/)). In design workshops, cartographers avoided free pan and zoom in narrative maps because panning "disrupts the page's flow" ([responsive thematic map workshops, arXiv 2407.20735](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.20735)). No controlled study compares scrollytelling maps with reader-driven maps on spatial comprehension. Treat both camps as craft wisdom.

**Uncertainty.** In a user study, **boundary fuzziness and colour lightness** were the preferred visual variables for uncertainty, among both GIS-trained and untrained students ([ISPRS IJGI 2014](https://doaj.org/article/be5bf23db49c4a299bcdf64749658af8)). Historical GIS work separates kinds of spatial uncertainty, and some medieval spaces were fuzzy even to contemporaries ([Breier, Digitising Patterns of Power](https://dpp.oeaw.ac.at/conference/abstract)). Bootstrapped "confidence-band" borders have been built from conflicting maps ([Fluid Borders](https://tandf.figshare.com/articles/Fluid_Borders_Rethinking_Historical_Geography_and_Fixed_Map_Boundaries_in_Contested_Regions/1493054/1)). For Maurya specifically, archaeologists model the empire as **nodes and corridors** (where edicts sit relative to population and routes) rather than a filled territory ([Smith et al., Antiquity 2016](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2m66x8f3)).

### Where the atlas stands (from `components/atlas/`)

These are already built: reader-driven camera; scrolling sets only `emphasis`; date chips set the year; claims pin places; one request per range so scrubbing only filters; Play, with reduced motion hopping between border changes; tweened highlight states; dashed reconstructed borders. The recommendations below build on that.

### Recommendations, prioritised

**P0: do next**

1. **Snap the scrubber to change points.** Cliopatria rows change at irregular steps: Maurya has 8 rows over 148 years, the Mughal Empire 51 over 330, and British territory 1757–1858 is nearly annual. Draw a tick at every year where a focus polity's row changes, let the drag snap within a few pixels of a tick, and make ←/→ jump to the previous or next change (with Shift for ±1 year and PageUp/PageDown for ±25). Follow the [WAI-ARIA slider pattern](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/slider/) and set `aria-valuetext` to "261 BCE". This is what makes scrubbing feel informative rather than jittery: every stop shows something new.
2. **Crossfade borders; never morph them.** Fade the outgoing row out and the incoming row in over about 200 ms (feature-state opacity, which `tween.ts` already supports). Interpolating polygon shapes would show territory nobody mapped. Under reduced motion, cut.
3. **Say which reconstruction, and for which years, on hover.** The tooltip or pin card should say: "Maurya Empire, as drawn by Cliopatria for 268–232 BCE. One reconstruction; borders not treaty-based." Cliopatria's own paper says the dataset "does not currently encode possible border uncertainty" and that users "may need to account for it" ([Bennett et al. 2025, Sci Data](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11822181/)). Repeating that sentence where the reader is looking costs nothing.
4. **Handle the three structural gaps in our data explicitly** (measured against our local v0.2.1 file, §2):
   - **1700–600 BCE has no polities.** Show a quiet empty state, "No state borders are known for this time. Places shown are archaeological sites and texts' regions", rather than a blank map.
   - **The Indus civilisation is one "polity" of 755,000 km² (2300–1801 BCE).** Draw it as a **culture area** (a hatched or lighter fill with a "culture, not a state" label) instead of an empire. Otherwise the map teaches the misconception that it was a single state.
   - **Princely states disappear into British territory.** Hyderabad has no row from 1799 to 1946, Kashmir none from 1885 to 1946, and Mysore ends in 1799. Until we add a source for them, the independence page's map must carry the caption "British India and the princely states are drawn as one; about two-fifths of the subcontinent was ruled indirectly." (The two-fifths figure needs a citation from a PD or OK source before it ships.)
5. **Link both ways between text and map.** Text → map exists (hover highlights, click pins). Add map → text: hovering or tapping a polity or place highlights its chips in the visible chapter and puts ticks in the scroll gutter where it is mentioned further down, as an "in this chapter" list. Spatial contiguity, keeping related words and pictures close, has a solid meta-analytic base, with larger effects for complex material and for transfer ([Schroeder & Cenkci 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9435-9)). Linking is the next best thing when the map cannot sit beside every sentence.
6. **Put the atlas state in the URL** (`?y=-260&pin=kalinga`). A claim, a debate or an MCP note can then link to an exact map state, as OWID's year-in-URL embeds do. It also gives the back button a meaning.

**P1: soon**

7. **Offer an opt-in "follow the text" mode.** It is off by default. When it is on, entering a chapter glides the year to the chapter's `when` and shows a pill: "Following the text · 261 BCE · Stop". Any scrubber touch turns it off. This keeps Kosara's control while giving the guided feel for readers who want it. The MSR study suggests the transition matters more than the control mode, so make the glide good.
8. **Onion-skin the previous state.** While scrubbing, or just after a jump, keep the previous row's outline as a faint hairline for about 1 s, and on a pin let the reader "compare with…" another year. Change is what a reader is looking for, and without a reference it is invisible.
9. **Label the turning points on the map, not just on the scrubber.** At a stop such as "Kalinga, c. 261 BCE", put a short leader-line label on the map at the place. Pudding's advice to replace hover with fixed annotations applies directly on mobile.
10. **Use one mobile atlas with three states.** (a) A **time bar**, about 56 px, sticky at the top, with the year and Play. (b) A **peek** map at about 40% of `window.innerHeight`, not `vh`, which opens on tapping the bar or a chip. (c) **Full-screen** as a sheet. There is no hover: a chip tap pins and opens the peek. Map gestures stay two-finger. The Pudding's stack-vs-keep rule: keep the map sticky only in chapters whose `when` spans a change of borders; otherwise show a static inline mini-map.
11. **Give the map a text twin.** Under the atlas, a collapsible "In 260 BCE: Maurya Empire (largest), Early Cholas, Early Pandyas, Anurādhapura… [list]", updated with the year. Screen-reader users get the map's content, and every reader gets a scannable legend.

**P2: later**

12. **Show alternative reconstructions where scholars disagree.** "Disagreement is data" should apply to borders. For Maurya, show the Cliopatria fill against an "edicts and routes" view (points and corridors only, after Smith et al. 2016). A two-way toggle labelled with each view's source teaches more than any single map.
13. **A "where am I in time" anchor.** Keep a thin master-timeline strip with 3–5 world anchors the learner already knows (Rome, the Han dynasty, the Prophet Muhammad, Columbus) under the period scrubber. `meanwhile` blocks exist; this keeps them visible while scrubbing. (See §4, feature D.)
14. **Search by place and time, OldMapsOnline-style**, on /places: "show me what was at Ujjain in 400 CE".
15. **Attach PD historical maps to a year**, as georeferenced overlays at low opacity (Joppen 1907, Imperial Gazetteer 1909). The reader can then compare our reconstruction with what a 1909 cartographer drew. Do this only with a georeferencing step we can audit.

### Licensing risks, in one place

1. **CC0 tags on archive.org uploads are uploader assertions, not facts.** Davies 1949 is tagged CC0 and is in copyright. Record our own reasoning per source (as `docs/standards.md` already requires).
2. **Indian copyright is life + 60.** Jadunath Sarkar (d. 1958) entered the Indian public domain in 2019. S. N. Sen (1890–1962, per archive.org; not independently confirmed) entered the Indian public domain in 2023. A pre-1931 US-PD work can still be in copyright in India. Record both statuses.
3. **Commons "history of India" maps are mostly modern user reconstructions.** Never treat them as evidence. Attribute their CC BY-SA licence if shown.
4. **OHM features traced from game maps or copyrighted atlases.** Check `source=` before importing.
5. **Cliopatria attribution** must stay on every map view (it already does). If we publish derived extents, state "simplified and clipped" (already in `ATTRIBUTION`).

## 3. Content roadmap

### Order, and why

A novice needs **chronological scaffolding first** (§4), then each region's big polity, with debates introduced once there is something to argue about. Pages already exist for Maurya and for 1857–1947; Gupta is in progress.

### Per-era sources, misconceptions and debates

Two research agents checked each Internet Archive item on 2026-10-17. They opened its metadata, downloaded the OCR text and searched it for a passage from the work. IDs resolve at `https://archive.org/details/<id>`. **Copyright test:** US PD if published before 1931 (1931 works enter on 2027-01-01; later foreign works may have restored US copyright, 95 years from publication). India PD at life + 60, or publication + 60 for government works. The links for misconceptions and debates are mostly Wikipedia, as pointers only. Before a page ships, replace each with the scholarship it summarises.

**Catalogue traps found:**

- `in.ernet.dli.2015.85216` is labelled Marshall vol. I but contains an unrelated work.
- `ainiakbarivolum00mubgoog` is labelled Blochmann 1873 but contains Jarrett vol. II (1891).
- Both `embassysirthoma0*roegoog` items are Roe vol. II.
- `mlbd.*` and 1960s–70s reprint scans add copyrighted front matter. Quote only the original text.
- DLI (`in.ernet.dli.*`) dates and OCR are unreliable. Check them per item.

## 4. Learning-science quick wins

The site already has FSRS cards (misconception true/false, prompt, "how well supported"), confidence calibration, and `already_know` and `meanwhile` blocks. These five features reuse that machinery.

| Feature                                                                 | What to build                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               | Evidence                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            | Caveat                                                                                                                                                                 |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **A. A pre-question at the top of each chapter**                        | One question before the chapter ("Who ruled Pāṭaliputra in 300 BCE?"). The reader guesses, possibly wrong, and the chapter answers it. Reveal the answer, with its evidence seal, at the point in the text where it is settled. Reuse the claim's `prompt`.                                                 | Pretesting improves later retention **even when the guesses are wrong**, provided the correct answer follows. Shown with text passages and video lectures ([Pan & Rivers 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13421-022-01392-1); Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009). Pretests interpolated between prose segments helped 24-hour recall ([2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00426-024-02042-8)).                                                                                             | Learners underrate it and prefer re-reading, so frame it as "Guess first", with no score shown.                                                                        |
| **B. Refutation structure for misconceptions**                          | Make the `misconceptions` block always render three parts: **the belief, stated plainly → "this is not supported" → the explanation, with evidence**. Lint: a misconception without a correcting claim already warns, so make the block refuse to render without one.                                       | Refutation texts (state the misconception, refute it, explain) outperform expository texts that only give the correct account ([Guzzetti et al. 1993 and Tippett 2010, via PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8784251); [Springer 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09656-z)).                                                                                                                                                                                           | Don't repeat the myth in a headline without the refutation next to it. The familiarity backfire is weaker than once thought, but the layout should still pair the two. |
| **C. A confidence rating before the reveal**                            | On misconception cards and pre-questions, ask "How sure are you?" (3 levels) **before** showing the answer. Then surface "You were sure, and it's a myth" moments, and schedule those cards sooner. Calibration tracking already exists; make it visible at the moment of feedback.                         | **Hypercorrection:** errors made with high confidence are _more_ likely to be corrected after feedback ([Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.27.6.1491)). It is probably attention-driven: surprising feedback gets attended to.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | The mechanism is debated. Keep the feedback short and salient.                                                                                                         |
| **D. Timelines as a persistent scaffold, with known anchors**           | Always show the period's position on a master timeline, with 3–5 **world anchors the learner already knows** (Rome, the Han dynasty, Muhammad, Columbus, the World Wars), plus the learner's own previously studied eras highlighted as "you've read this". The anchors become the default `meanwhile` set. | Chronological understanding improves with **explicit, consistent use of good timelines**, in a curriculum-level comparison and an intervention study ("Timewise") ([de Groot-Reuvekamp et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/00220272.2013.869837); [EUROCLIO summary](https://euroclio.eu/2015/09/04/new-article-on-the-understanding-of-historical-time/)). Advance organisers work best when they **link the familiar to the unfamiliar** ([updated meta-analysis](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/20405)). | The timeline evidence is from primary schools. For adults this is plausible extrapolation, not proven.                                                                 |
| **E. A two-minute retrieval check at each chapter's end, then spacing** | At a chapter's end: 2–3 free-recall prompts drawn from the chapter's claims (without the text visible), then "Add to my reviews". The FSRS deck takes over from there. Include a **map-recall** card: "Tap where Kalinga was", answered on the atlas.                                                       | Practice testing beats re-studying ([Adesope et al. 2017 meta-analysis, 272 effect sizes](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654316689306)). Practice testing and distributed practice are the only two techniques rated "high utility" ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)). The optimal gap grows with the retention interval ([Cepeda et al. 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)).                     | Free recall beats recognition, but is harder to grade without a model. Self-grading against the claim text is fine for an MVP.                                         |

**One warning for the image work: seductive details.** Interesting but irrelevant images and anecdotes reduce learning, more so when static and combined with text. That is the format of a hero image ([Sundararajan & Adesope 2020](https://news.wsu.edu/press-release/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/), g ≈ −0.33 per secondary summaries). Every Commons image should **carry a claim** ("Aśoka's Lion Capital, Sarnath: the edicts' pillars looked like this") and sit next to the text it supports (spatial contiguity, §1 rec. 5). Don't use mood images.
